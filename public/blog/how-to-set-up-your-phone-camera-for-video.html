How to set up your phone camera for the best possible video

Your phone camera is fine. What ruins most phone video is the phone making decisions halfway through the take.

It brightens the picture, then darkens it. It hunts for focus. The colours drift from warm to cold because someone walked past a window. None of that is your fault, and all of it can be switched off before you press record.

This is the deep dive on the camera side of our phone video tips. We'll cover the settings to change once, the three things to lock on every shoot (focus, exposure and white balance), and how to do it on an iPhone and on Android. It takes about ten minutes to set up. It saves hours in the edit.

Why auto mode ruins good footage

Your phone is built to get a good photo. Video is a different job. A photo is one frame, so the camera can take its time. Video is thousands of frames, and if the camera keeps re-judging the scene, you see every re-judgement.

Three things drift while you record:

  • Exposure. You raise a hand, a cloud passes, and the brightness pumps up and down.
  • Focus. The camera decides the plant behind you is more interesting than your face, and the picture goes soft.
  • White balance. The camera guesses the colour of the light, keeps changing its mind, and your skin tone swings from orange to blue mid-sentence.

Any one of these is annoying. All three together make footage that looks like it was filmed by accident. They're also the hardest problems to fix afterwards, because you can't colour grade a clip that changes colour halfway through. Lock them, and you grade once and it stays graded.

Settings to change once, before you shoot

On an iPhone, most of these live in Settings, then Camera, then Record Video. Change them once and forget about them.

  1. Shoot in 4K. 4K at 30 frames per second is a solid default for social and ads. Even if you export in HD, you get a sharper picture and room to crop and reframe. You can also flip between HD and 4K, and between 24, 30 and 60 fps, with the toggles in the top right of the camera screen in Video mode.
  2. Pick one frame rate and stick to it. 24 fps looks more filmic, 30 is the all-rounder, 60 is for smooth motion or slow-down in the edit. Mixing frame rates in one project causes headaches, so choose before you start.
  3. Turn Auto FPS off. In low light, the phone can quietly drop to 24 fps to get a brighter picture. Handy for a one-off, but it means your frame rate changes without you asking.
  4. Turn HDR Video off, unless you know your edit handles it. HDR footage can be harder to edit, and mixing HDR and standard clips in one project can leave you with colour and brightness that don't match.
  5. Switch on Lock Camera. On supported iPhones this stops the phone swapping between lenses mid-recording, which shows up as a sudden jump in the picture.
  6. Use the main lens and leave stabilisation on. The main camera is usually the best one you have. Skip the digital zoom and move closer instead.
  7. Turn the grid on (Settings, then Camera, then Grid). It helps you keep the phone level and your framing consistent.

On Android the menus have different names, but the same options exist. Look in the camera's video settings for resolution, frame rate, HDR and stabilisation. Samsung Galaxy phones also have a Pro Video mode under More, which we'll come back to.

How to lock focus and exposure

This is the single biggest upgrade you can make, and it costs nothing.

On an iPhone

  1. Open Camera and switch to Video.
  2. Frame the shot exactly as it will look when you're recording.
  3. Tap and hold on your face (or on the spot where you'll be standing) until a yellow AE/AF Lock banner appears at the top of the screen.
  4. Slide up or down on the little sun icon beside the focus box to brighten or darken the picture.
  5. Record. To unlock, tap anywhere on the screen.

The lock cancels when you close the Camera app, so set it every time you start a new shoot.

If you're filming yourself, put something in your spot first, like a chair or a bag with a face-height object on it, lock on that, then step in. That way the phone isn't locked on the wall behind you.

On the iPhone 16 range you can also use Camera Control. Turn on Lock Focus and Exposure under Settings, Camera, Camera Control, then lightly press and hold the button until AE/AF Lock appears. The catch is that the lock only lasts while you keep your finger on it, so it suits handheld shots more than self-filming.

If you want more control than the stock Camera app gives you, try Apple's free Final Cut Camera app. Touch and hold on your subject to lock focus and exposure (the same yellow AE/AF Lock appears, and you tap anywhere to unlock). It also has manual focus, white balance and stabilisation controls.

On Android

Samsung Galaxy phones have a Pro Video mode (swipe to More, then Pro Video). It lets you set ISO, exposure value, white balance and colour tone by hand, and there's a manual focus slider. Many other Android cameras lock focus and exposure with a long press on the screen, but it varies by phone, so test yours before a proper shoot.

Two things to remember

  • Locked focus stays put. If you move closer to or further from the phone, you'll drift out of focus. Mark your spot and stay on it.
  • Locked exposure doesn't react. If the light changes (sun goes in, someone opens a door), the picture won't adjust. Check it every few takes.

Quick test: record ten seconds, wave a hand in front of the lens, and play it back. If the brightness pumps, the lock didn't take.

How to lock white balance

White balance is the phone's guess at what colour your light is, so it knows what counts as white. Warm bulbs push the picture orange, shade and overcast skies push it blue, and in auto mode the phone keeps correcting as things change. Lock it and the colour stays put.

On an iPhone

The stock camera has a built-in lock, and plenty of people don't know it's there. On iOS 17 and later, go to Settings, then Camera, then Record Video, and turn on Lock White Balance.

With that on, the phone works out the white balance at the start of each clip and holds it until you stop. Two things follow from that:

  • Get the light and framing right before you press record, because that's the moment it measures.
  • It's an automatic lock, not a manual control. You can't choose a colour temperature in the stock app.

When you want to choose the colour yourself

For manual control, use a camera app built for video. Blackmagic Camera is free on iPhone and Android, and lets you set white balance to locked, auto or manual (roughly 2500K to 10,000K), with presets for daylight, cloud, shade, fluorescent and incandescent light. It also has a Lock White Balance on Record option. Apple's free Final Cut Camera app has a white balance control too.

On Samsung Galaxy phones, Pro Video mode lets you set the colour temperature by hand.

Pick the preset that matches your light, record a few seconds, and check that skin looks natural on playback. If it looks too orange or too blue, change it and test again.

The easy win: don't mix your light

The cleanest fix is to give the phone one type of light. A window on one side and a warm lamp on the other pulls the colour two ways, and no lock will make that look good. Switch the lamp off, or close the curtains and light the room properly. Daylight from a window is free and usually the best you'll get.

Shutter speed and ISO: the next level

The stock iPhone camera doesn't let you set shutter speed or ISO. Samsung's Pro Video mode and apps like Blackmagic Camera do. You don't need this for most talking-head or social video, and the three locks above get you most of the way. If you want smoother, more natural-looking motion, here's the rule.

Set your shutter speed to roughly double your frame rate. It's called the 180 degree shutter rule, and it gives the amount of motion blur our eyes expect.

Frame rateShutter speed
24 fps1/48 (often set to 1/50)
30 fps1/60
60 fps1/120

Too fast and movement looks choppy and jittery. Too slow and everything smears. Some apps let you set a shutter angle (180 degrees) instead of a speed, which does the same job whatever frame rate you pick.

In bright sunlight, holding 1/60 can overexpose the shot, because there's too much light getting in. A neutral density (ND) filter that clips over the lens is the usual fix.

ISO is how sensitive the sensor is to light. Keep it as low as the scene allows. The higher it goes, the grainier the picture gets. If you're hitting high ISO, the answer is almost always more light, not more settings.

Your pre-shoot checklist

Set these once:

  • 4K, one frame rate, Auto FPS off, HDR Video off, Lock Camera on
  • Lock White Balance on (iPhone), or a manual app if you want to pick the colour yourself
  • Grid on

Do these every shoot:

  • Wipe the lens and turn on Do Not Disturb
  • Set up your light: face a window, kill any mixed lighting
  • Frame the shot, then tap and hold to lock focus and exposure
  • Record ten seconds, play it back, and check the sound, brightness and colour before you carry on
